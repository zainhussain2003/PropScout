# PropScout investor underwriting method

## Executive finding

PropScout’s current investor report is best understood as a **leveraged rental-income screen for a small Ontario landlord**. It answers whether market rent covers estimated operating costs and mortgage debt at today’s price and financing. That is a useful question, but it is narrower than the report’s name suggests.

The current deal score does not account for an investor who knowingly accepts negative short-term cash flow in exchange for mortgage principal repayment and possible long-term appreciation. The report displays a 20-year equity chart, but appreciation and principal paydown do not affect the backend verdict. The displayed equity return also omits cumulative cash shortfalls, selling costs, tax, refinancing, rate renewals and capital expenditures. It therefore cannot yet establish that an appreciation-led hold is attractive.

The score should not be loosened. The recommended product method is to separate three questions:

1. **Do the property’s rental operations work?** This is the existing income-fundamentals analysis.
2. **Can this investor safely carry the financing?** This depends on the investor’s liquidity, other income, debt and downside capacity.
3. **Does the full hold produce an adequate risk-adjusted return?** This requires scenario-based total-return analysis, including every cash contribution and the net proceeds of sale.

An appreciation-led investor should be allowed to receive a conclusion such as **“Negative-cash-flow hold · viable only under the 3% growth case”**. The product should not turn that case into “Strong deal” unless it remains resilient under conservative assumptions and the appreciation evidence is locally sourced.

## The investor the current report actually serves

The current inputs and sections reveal a specific audience:

- an individual buying one Ontario residential property;
- a condo, house, duplex, triplex or fourplex rather than a large apartment operation;
- long-term rental use rather than flipping, development or an actively modelled short-term rental;
- conventional residential mortgage financing;
- an investor choosing a down payment, mortgage rate and amortization;
- a buyer who needs market rent, operating costs, cash flow, DSCR, closing cash and basic risk flags.

This is a sensible initial market. It includes first-time landlords, small portfolio investors and owner-occupiers considering a two-to-four-unit property. CMHC’s rental-income guidance distinguishes owner-occupied and non-owner-occupied two-to-four-unit properties and allows different gross- or net-rental-income treatments in debt-service calculations.^1 OSFI also states that rental income may enter debt-service calculations when lenders apply rigorous due diligence.^2 PropScout’s simplified qualification card cannot substitute for lender underwriting, but this audience is coherent.

The report is not presently adequate for:

- five-plus-unit commercial-style properties that require a verified operating statement and commercial financing;
- renovation or BRRRR strategies requiring renovation scope, after-repair value and refinance proceeds;
- flips requiring transaction timing, selling costs, business-income tax treatment and construction risk;
- development or conversion projects requiring zoning, permits, schedules and cost contingencies;
- short-term rental underwriting without real occupancy, rate, fee and municipal-compliance data;
- portfolio allocation decisions involving concentration, liquidity and correlations across multiple properties.

Those strategies should remain outside the initial verdict until their required data exists.

## How real-estate investors evaluate a hold

### Property operations

The first layer evaluates the asset before financing:

- gross potential rent;
- vacancy and bad debt;
- other income;
- property tax;
- insurance;
- utilities paid by the landlord;
- repairs and routine maintenance;
- property management and leasing;
- condominium fees where applicable;
- an allowance for replacement reserves and capital work;
- net operating income;
- cap rate and gross rent multiplier.

This layer asks whether the property itself produces durable income. Cap rate is useful because it separates property operations from a particular buyer’s mortgage. RICS describes both income-capitalization and explicit discounted-cash-flow approaches for investment property, with DCF converting future cash flows into current value.^3

PropScout covers much of this layer, but several expenses are fixed estimates rather than evidence. It uses 0.35% of property value for insurance, a build-year maintenance percentage, 5% vacancy, and an optional 8% management fee. Missing utilities, leasing costs, bad debt, capital work and special assessments can materially overstate NOI.

### Financing and liquidity

The second layer applies the buyer’s financing:

- down payment and acquisition costs;
- mortgage payment and interest rate;
- principal repayment;
- cash flow after debt service;
- DSCR;
- cash-on-cash return;
- rate-renewal and mortgage stress cases;
- the investor’s other debts and income;
- accessible reserves for vacancy, repairs and monthly shortfalls.

Mortgage principal repayment is a cash outflow, but it is not an operating expense. It converts liquid cash into property equity. CRA does not permit mortgage principal to be deducted as a rental expense, while qualifying interest may be deductible.^4 FCAC similarly explains that home equity can grow through mortgage repayment or an increase in property value.^5

This distinction explains why a sophisticated investor may tolerate negative cash flow. A $1,000 monthly shortfall is not automatically a $12,000 annual economic loss if the mortgage balance falls by more than $12,000. It is still a $12,000 annual **liquidity requirement**, and the equity is illiquid and exposed to property-price and transaction-cost risk.

### Total return over the hold

The third layer measures all cash flows through disposition:

- initial down payment and acquisition costs;
- every monthly or annual cash contribution;
- rent and expense growth;
- vacancy and capital expenditures;
- mortgage principal reduction;
- refinancing proceeds or costs;
- property appreciation or decline;
- selling costs;
- net mortgage payoff;
- tax consequences;
- net present value, internal rate of return and equity multiple.

Professional real-estate performance separates income return from capital growth and combines both into total return.^6 An explicit DCF makes the assumed rental growth, capital growth, exit value and discount rate visible instead of hiding growth inside a single cap rate.^3

This is the correct home for an appreciation-led thesis. Appreciation should be a scenario input rather than a guaranteed score contribution.

### Evidence and execution risk

The fourth layer asks how much of the model is known:

- Are rents executed leases, seller statements or comparable asking rents?
- Are expenses supported by bills and a trailing operating statement?
- Is the rental use legal?
- Is rent control known?
- Are inspection, status-certificate, reserve-fund and special-assessment risks known?
- Is the appreciation series local, property-type-specific and current?
- Does the investor have enough reserves to survive the stress case?

Evidence quality should constrain the strength of the recommendation. It should not be blended into economic return as if a missing lease were simply two fewer points.

## Investor strategies PropScout should distinguish

### Income-first investor

Objective: durable current income with limited reliance on appreciation.

The property should normally have positive cash flow, adequate DSCR, acceptable cap rate and a positive stress case. Appreciation is upside. This is the strategy most closely matched by the existing deal score.

### Balanced buy-and-hold investor

Objective: combine income, principal repayment and conservative long-term capital growth.

Some modest negative cash flow can be rational when principal repayment offsets it, reserves are strong, the hold is long enough to absorb transaction costs, and the flat or low-growth scenario remains tolerable. The primary outputs should be total cash contributed, principal repaid, break-even appreciation, downside loss and scenario IRR.

This should be PropScout’s default investor strategy because it accommodates both disciplined cash-flow buyers and ordinary long-term holders without assuming speculative growth.

### Appreciation-led investor

Objective: accept a known carrying cost to gain leveraged exposure to a particular local market.

This is a legitimate strategy, but it is materially more sensitive to price assumptions, financing renewals and forced-sale risk. The Bank of Canada has noted that expectations of price increases can attract investors and amplify housing-price cycles.^7 It also cautions that investor activity and extrapolative expectations can increase vulnerability when prices become detached from fundamentals.^8

PropScout should report this as a distinct thesis, not improve the income score. A suitable conclusion is “speculative hold” or “appreciation-dependent,” accompanied by the exact growth rate required to break even.

### House hacker or owner-occupier investor

Objective: occupy part of a two-to-four-unit property while tenants offset housing costs.

This combines personal-use affordability and investment economics. The report should calculate the owner’s net housing cost and the rental portion separately. Qualification and tax treatment differ from a fully non-owner-occupied rental, and CMHC’s rental-income rules distinguish these cases.^1

### Value-add investor

Objective: create value through renovation, rent stabilization, conversion or improved operations.

This strategy needs a verified scope, budget, contingency, timeline, legal approvals, post-renovation rent and after-repair value. Until those inputs exist, PropScout should show “value-add case unavailable” rather than infer upside from listing language.

## Does negative cash flow necessarily make a bad investment?

No. It makes the investment dependent on other return sources and on the investor’s ability to fund the shortfall.

The economic identity is:

> Total investment result = rental cash flows + principal repaid + net appreciation − acquisition costs − sale costs − capital costs − tax

Principal repayment matters, but it should not be mistaken for income. Appreciation matters, but it is uncertain and cannot be spent without selling or borrowing. Negative cash flow matters because the investor must fund it regardless of what the property may eventually be worth.

### Illustration: $1,000 monthly shortfall

The following sensitivity uses a $729,900 property, 20% down, a 4.79% 25-year mortgage, $13,473 of acquisition costs, a constant $1,000 monthly shortfall and an assumed 5% selling cost. It excludes tax, capital work, rent growth, refinancing and rate changes. These are illustrative assumptions, not a forecast.

The mortgage payment is approximately $3,327/month. About $12,494 of principal is repaid in year one, slightly more than the $12,000 cash shortfall. That does not make the investment automatically profitable because the investor also paid acquisition and eventual selling costs.

| Hold     | Appreciation | Cumulative shortfall | Net profit after assumed sale costs | Approx. annualized IRR |
| -------- | -----------: | -------------------: | ----------------------------------: | ---------------------: |
| 5 years  |           0% |              $60,000 |                            −$41,139 |                 −4.76% |
| 5 years  |           2% |              $60,000 |                            +$31,031 |                  3.10% |
| 5 years  |           3% |              $60,000 |                            +$69,302 |                  6.50% |
| 5 years  |           5% |              $60,000 |                           +$150,436 |                 12.58% |
| 10 years |           0% |             $120,000 |                            −$13,931 |                 −0.65% |
| 10 years |           2% |             $120,000 |                           +$137,921 |                  5.10% |
| 10 years |           3% |             $120,000 |                           +$224,542 |                  7.50% |
| 10 years |           5% |             $120,000 |                           +$422,148 |                 11.72% |

This example supports the investor’s intuition: a $1,000 monthly shortfall can coexist with a positive long-term return. It also shows why PropScout cannot answer the question from cash flow alone. At zero appreciation, the same hold loses money after transaction costs. At 2–5% appreciation, the result changes sharply.

### The Buttermill-scale shortfall

Applying the same illustration to a $2,724 monthly shortfall produces a different conclusion:

| Hold     | Appreciation | Net profit after assumed sale costs | Approx. annualized IRR |
| -------- | -----------: | ----------------------------------: | ---------------------: |
| 5 years  |           3% |                            −$34,138 |                 −3.00% |
| 5 years  |           5% |                            +$46,996 |                  3.68% |
| 10 years |           3% |                            +$17,662 |                  0.54% |
| 10 years |           5% |                           +$215,268 |                  5.46% |

Even before tax and capital expenditures, a 3% growth assumption barely rescues the ten-year case. A 5% annual growth assumption is required to produce a moderate return. That is exactly the kind of dependence PropScout should expose. The current hard-pass result remains reasonable as an income verdict, while the long-term panel should explain the appreciation hurdle rather than claim that appreciation is irrelevant.

## Current PropScout behaviour

### What is already useful

- The backend calculates NOI, cap rate, cash flow, cash-on-cash return, DSCR, GRM and break-even rent deterministically.
- The mortgage engine uses Canadian semi-annual compounding.
- The report collects down payment, rate and amortization assumptions.
- It shows a 20-year equity curve with selectable 0%, 2%, 3% and 5% appreciation cases.
- It exposes the OSFI stress-test rate and risk flags.
- Severe risks can cap the investment score.

### What the verdict measures

The score assigns 25 points to cap rate, 25 to monthly cash flow, 20 to cash-on-cash return, 15 to DSCR and 10 to rental demand. Appreciation, principal repayment, hold duration, sale proceeds, investor reserves and total-return targets receive no points.

This makes the verdict an income-and-debt-coverage verdict. That is defensible if it is labelled accurately. It is not a complete investment verdict.

### Score saturation

Cap rate, cash flow, cash-on-cash return and DSCR are not independent. The same favourable rent-to-price relationship drives all four. Once cap rate reaches 6%, cash flow reaches $500/month, cash-on-cash reaches 8%, and DSCR reaches 1.25×, all 85 financial points are awarded. Default demand inputs then commonly produce 92 raw points and 97 displayed points.

The current Hamilton calibration found four materially different properties at exactly 92 raw / 97 displayed. Their evidence ranged from a seller-stated NOI that nearly reconciled to the engine to a nine-unit property without unit-level income or expenses. The score cannot express that difference.

### Equity-chart limitation

The browser equity calculation is:

> projected property value − projected mortgage balance

Its displayed return compares equity growth with initial cash invested. It does not subtract the investor’s cumulative negative cash flow. It also omits acquisition and sale costs from the property-level equity line, tax, capital expenditures, refinancing, rate renewal, rent and expense growth, and inflation.

The default 3% appreciation rate is a user-adjustable scenario, but the current UI can look like a forecast. No local Ontario sales series is connected. Statistics Canada’s residential price-index methodology illustrates why a defensible appreciation series requires transaction data and constant-quality controls rather than a simple national average.^9

### Financing interaction limitation

The live report keeps the backend score fixed while the financing controls change. The current `enrichMetrics` path builds expenses, OSFI and equity displays around the saved API metrics; it does not re-run the authoritative backend score for each scenario. A user can therefore change financing while continuing to see the original headline grade.

That behaviour avoids frontend/backend score drift, but it means scenario changes must be clearly labelled as exploratory until a backend scenario endpoint returns recalculated metrics and verdicts.

### Market-data limitation

The application’s Hamilton vacancy table currently contains 3.3% and labels its city values as indicative placeholders. CMHC’s 2025 Rental Market Report gives a 3.6% Hamilton CMA purpose-built vacancy rate and notes weaker student demand in West End and Mountain zones, while condominium apartments had a 1.2% vacancy rate.^10 A single city-wide number is therefore inadequate for every Hamilton property type and submarket.

## Recommended PropScout method

### One report, three conclusions

The investor report should produce three independent results:

| Result              | Question                                                  | Typical output                                            |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Income fundamentals | Does the rental operation work at today’s price?          | Strong / adequate / weak / fails                          |
| Hold-case return    | What happens to all invested cash over the selected hold? | Scenario IRR, equity multiple and break-even appreciation |
| Evidence confidence | How much of the result is verified?                       | High / medium / low / insufficient                        |

The top verdict can then read:

> **Fails as an income property. Appreciation-led hold only. Low evidence confidence.**

That sentence is more informative than forcing every strategy into one number.

### Strategy selection

Before analysis, ask one question:

> What is your main plan for this property?

- **Income now** — prioritize current cash flow and downside coverage.
- **Balanced long-term hold** — weigh income, principal repayment and conservative growth scenarios.
- **Appreciation-led hold** — accept a selected monthly contribution and test the required growth rate.
- **Live in part, rent part** — calculate personal housing cost and rental economics separately.

Changing strategy should change the conclusion and emphasized metrics. It should not change known property facts or manufacture a better score.

### Income-fundamentals result

Retain the current property metrics, but:

- rename the score from “Deal score” to **“Income fundamentals”** until total return is integrated;
- use continuous component curves instead of large step thresholds;
- reduce duplicated rewards from four highly correlated metrics;
- use the conservative expense case for the headline;
- show the self-managed case as an investor-controlled scenario;
- treat five-plus-unit properties as unsupported until operating-statement inputs exist;
- keep severe risk gates.

No component weights should be finalized before calibration against a documented set of real Ontario properties.

### Hold-case engine

Add an explicit annual or monthly cash-flow model with:

- selected hold period;
- starting rent and a sourced or user-entered rent-growth assumption;
- operating expenses and expense growth;
- vacancy and leasing costs;
- capital-expenditure schedule and contingency;
- mortgage term, amortization and renewal-rate scenarios;
- principal repayment;
- appreciation scenarios of flat, local historical reference, and user case;
- disposition costs and mortgage discharge;
- cumulative investor contributions;
- pre-tax IRR, NPV, equity multiple and break-even appreciation;
- optional tax-sensitive view only after collecting the required tax inputs.

CRA distinguishes current expenses from capital expenses and requires rental income and expenses to be reported through Form T776.^11 A rental-property sale can create a capital gain and potentially CCA consequences.^12 PropScout should therefore label its first total-return model “pre-tax” and avoid estimating after-tax proceeds without ownership, income, adjusted-cost-base and CCA inputs.

### Downside and carrying-capacity gate

An appreciation-led property should be eligible for analysis only after the investor supplies:

- maximum comfortable monthly contribution;
- accessible reserve amount;
- hold period;
- other debt payments and household income;
- expected major work;
- whether rent is contracted or estimated.

The report should calculate:

- months of shortfall covered by reserves;
- flat-price outcome;
- lower-rent and higher-expense case;
- mortgage-renewal shock;
- break-even sale price and annual appreciation rate;
- loss if forced to sell after one, three or five years.

The mortgage stress test remains a qualification screen. Federally regulated lenders generally use the higher of 5.25% or the contract rate plus two percentage points, and they also consider other debts.^13 A PropScout pass must continue to say that it is an estimate, not lender approval.

### Evidence-confidence gate

| Confidence   | Minimum evidence                                                                        | Recommendation ceiling           |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Insufficient | Missing rent or a material expense with no defensible range                             | No score or verdict              |
| Low          | Seller-stated income, weak asking-rent proxies, estimated expenses                      | Caution / appreciation-dependent |
| Medium       | Relevant individual comps or disclosed leases, taxes known, major expenses bounded      | Good deal                        |
| High         | Rent roll or leases, trailing operating statement, verified taxes and material expenses | Strong deal                      |

The economic score may still be shown above the ceiling as “projected economics,” but the actionable verdict must respect the evidence limit.

### Appreciation rules

Appreciation should enter the product under these rules:

1. **Never use an appreciation assumption in the income-fundamentals score.**
2. **Default the hold-case comparison to 0% rather than 3%.** The user can select additional scenarios.
3. **Label every rate as flat, sourced historical reference or user assumption.**
4. **Use local, property-type-specific history when licensed Ontario data becomes available.**
5. **Show a range and drawdown history, not only a compound average.**
6. **Calculate the appreciation rate required to hit the investor’s target return.**
7. **Never call historical appreciation a forecast.** The Bank of Canada notes that survey expectations can differ substantially from published price-index growth.^14

## Recommended verdict language

### Income-first pass

> **Works on current income.** Rent covers the conservative operating case and debt service, including management. The stress case remains positive. Verify the rent roll and expenses before relying on the result.

### Balanced hold

> **Marginal income, plausible long-term hold.** The property requires approximately $450/month in the base case. Mortgage paydown offsets part of that contribution, but the flat-price case loses money after transaction costs. At 2% annual appreciation, the modeled ten-year pre-tax return is X%. Appreciation is an assumption, not a forecast.

### Appreciation-dependent

> **Fails on current income.** The investment requires $1,000/month and at least X% annual appreciation over ten years to reach the selected return target. It loses $Y in the flat-price case. Proceed only if that contribution and downside fit your plan.

### Insufficient evidence

> **No reliable verdict.** The listing provides no rent roll and material operating expenses are unknown. Add leases or comparable rents and actual expenses before underwriting the hold.

## Product recommendation

PropScout should be curated first for **small Ontario long-term buy-and-hold investors evaluating one-to-four-unit residential properties**. The default strategy should be Balanced long-term hold, with Income now, Appreciation-led hold and Live in part/rent part as explicit alternatives.

The current score should remain conservative and be relabelled as income fundamentals. The immediate redesign should add evidence confidence and a conservative scenario. The next major capability should be a pre-tax hold-case engine that includes cumulative cash contributions and net sale proceeds. Continuous scoring and new weights should follow only after calibration on real Ontario listings and, once licensed, Ontario sold-price history.

This structure respects both investor realities:

- negative cash flow does not automatically make a long-term investment irrational; and
- hoped-for appreciation does not make an unaffordable or poorly evidenced property a strong deal.

## Proposed implementation sequence after approval

1. Define strategy and evidence-confidence schemas without changing calculation outputs.
2. Relabel the present score as Income fundamentals and expose its complete breakdown.
3. Add the evidence-confidence ceiling and insufficient-data state.
4. Make the conservative operating scenario the primary investor view.
5. Add a deterministic pre-tax hold-case calculator with cumulative cash flows, sale costs, IRR, equity multiple and break-even appreciation.
6. Add strategy-specific verdict prose generated from structured outputs.
7. Add the investor liquidity and reserve inputs needed for appreciation-led cases.
8. Replace the browser-only equity curve with backend scenario results.
9. Calibrate continuous component curves on real Ontario examples.
10. Add licensed local appreciation evidence when the Ontario/TRREB data source is available.

No implementation should begin until the owner approves this product method and the associated evidence rules.

## Review feedback and revision notes

Added 2026-09-10 to preserve the independent review of this research. These notes are feedback for the next design pass, not approved implementation decisions.

### What the review verified

The reviewer checked the research against the current code and independently recomputed its illustrative finance cases. The following claims were confirmed:

- the score weights are cap rate 25, cash flow 25, cash-on-cash return 20, DSCR 15 and market demand 10;
- the component maximum is 95, so a raw score of 92 displays as 97 after normalization;
- the Hamilton vacancy constant is 3.3% and is labelled in code as an indicative placeholder;
- the browser enrichment layer preserves the backend headline score;
- projected equity is property value minus remaining mortgage balance and does not deduct cumulative cash shortfalls;
- the insurance, vacancy, management and age-based maintenance assumptions described above match the code; and
- the mortgage payment, first-year principal repayment, profit and IRR examples reproduce when calculated independently using Canadian semi-annual mortgage compounding. Principal repayment is not double-counted: it leaves through mortgage payments and returns through the lower mortgage payoff at sale.

The reviewer agreed with the central rule that appreciation must never increase the income-fundamentals score.

### Issues raised by the review

1. **The ten-year illustrations combine inconsistent static assumptions.** The examples hold the monthly cash shortfall flat while the property price compounds. Omitting rent growth understates the appreciation scenarios, while holding a 4.79% mortgage rate for ten years ignores renewal risk and can overstate them. The latter may be the larger effect. The Buttermill ten-year, 3% appreciation result of a 0.54% IRR is therefore illustrative and should not carry a product conclusion by itself.

2. **A 0% appreciation case is a stress case, not a neutral forecast.** Flat nominal value for ten years implies a decline in real purchasing-power terms if inflation remains positive. It remains a useful default comparison, but should be labelled `Flat nominal · stress case`.

3. **The correlated score components need to be separated by purpose.** Cap rate measures unlevered property economics. DSCR measures financing resilience. Monthly cash flow measures the investor's liquidity burden. Cash-on-cash return is closely correlated with leveraged cash flow in the present implementation and is the better candidate to remove from the headline score while retaining it as a displayed metric. Simply reducing all four metrics would discard useful information about leverage. Absolute cash-flow thresholds also compare properties of very different size poorly and require calibration.

4. **The proposed evidence ceiling would make the primary address-input path structurally incapable of returning a good verdict.** Address-entered analyses normally begin with estimated rent and estimated expenses, which would qualify as low confidence. This is a major product decision. A better presentation is to keep the projected economic result visible and independently state its evidence confidence, for example `Strong projected economics · low evidence confidence`. A stronger phrase such as `Strong verified deal` should require leases, taxes and material expenses to be verified. Missing material inputs can still prevent a reliable verdict.

5. **A mandatory strategy question would add friction to the existing mode selection.** The product already asks whether the property is for investment or personal use, and the minimal input flow is deliberate. The investment report should initially open in a Balanced hold view and let the user change strategy inside the completed report rather than adding another blocking modal step.

6. **Renaming Deal score has a wider migration cost than the proposed sequence suggests.** The term appears in the product specification, database score versioning, PDF output and landing-page examples as well as the report UI. The safer first step is to retain `Deal score` and add the qualifier `Current-income case`. Any later rename should be a versioned product and data-contract migration.

7. **The external figures carrying product decisions must remain directly verifiable.** The cited CMHC Hamilton report supports a 2025 purpose-built rental vacancy rate of 3.6% and a condominium-apartment vacancy rate of 1.2%. The Bank of Canada source supports the warning that survey price expectations can differ from published price-index growth. The meaningful calibration issue is property-type segmentation, not the small difference between the current 3.3% Hamilton placeholder and the 3.6% purpose-built figure; both fall in the same current demand-score bracket.

### Revised direction for a future proposal

Before implementation, the next proposal should incorporate these revisions:

- model rent growth, operating-expense growth and mortgage renewal as separate scenario inputs;
- label 0% appreciation as a flat-nominal stress case;
- retain cap rate for the asset, DSCR for financing and monthly cash flow for liquidity, while moving cash-on-cash return out of the headline score unless calibration provides a reason to keep it;
- separate projected economics from evidence confidence instead of automatically capping every low-confidence address analysis at Caution;
- default the completed investor report to Balanced hold and put strategy switching inside the report;
- retain the Deal score name initially, qualified as the Current-income case; and
- segment vacancy assumptions by property type and verify every market assumption before it affects a verdict.

No code change or final product decision is authorized by this feedback section.
